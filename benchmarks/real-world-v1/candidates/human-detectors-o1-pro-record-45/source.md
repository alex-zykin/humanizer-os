In the wake of a major hurricane, as floodwaters recede and communities begin the long road to recovery, a new concern may arise for some electric vehicle owners: the risk of their car batteries catching fire. In recent years, reports have surfaced of electric vehicles (E.V.s) experiencing battery fires after being submerged in saltwater floods. While this scenario might sound alarming, experts say it remains a very rare occurrence and can generally be managed with proper precautions.

**Why Saltwater Poses a Risk**  
Under normal conditions, E.V. batteries—usually large lithium-ion packs—are carefully sealed and protected from moisture and debris. Electric cars go through rigorous testing to ensure they can handle various weather conditions, including heavy rain. But submersion in saltwater is an entirely different matter. The saline solution conducts electricity exceptionally well, which can create internal short circuits if it penetrates the car’s battery pack.

“Saltwater is a significant electrical conductor, and when it comes into direct contact with the battery’s internal components, it can potentially lead to a dangerous chemical reaction,” explains Dr. Hannah Morgan, a materials science expert at the University of Michigan’s Battery Lab. “This can result in what’s known as thermal runaway—a condition where the battery cells overheat, release gases, and potentially catch fire.”

Such incidents garnered attention after Hurricanes Ian and Sandy, when a handful of electric cars that had been soaked in saltwater ignited days—even weeks—later. The fires were difficult to extinguish and sometimes required large quantities of water and special training to control. These cases quickly made headlines, prompting questions about the safety of E.V.s in flood-prone areas.

**Putting the Risk in Perspective**  
While it’s true that E.V. battery fires can occur under these extreme circumstances, experts stress that these incidents remain rare. Electric vehicles are no more likely to catch fire than their gasoline-powered counterparts, and under typical conditions, they can be even less prone to ignition. The key difference is that when an E.V. fire does occur, it can be more challenging for first responders to manage, largely because of the unique nature of lithium-ion battery chemistry.

“Gasoline cars catch fire every day, often due to fuel leaks, electrical malfunctions, or engine issues,” says Michael Conway, a fire safety specialist with the National Fire Protection Association. “With E.V.s, the rate of fires is comparable or lower. The problem is that when we do see battery fires triggered by saltwater exposure, they can reignite multiple times due to the battery’s stored energy.”

**Post-Hurricane Safety Steps**  
For electric vehicle owners living in hurricane-prone regions, the best course of action is to take preventive measures. As a storm approaches, it’s wise to move your E.V. to higher ground or store it in a garage or location not vulnerable to flooding. If this isn’t possible, paying close attention to local evacuation orders and moving your vehicle out of harm’s way well in advance can make all the difference.

“If your E.V. does get submerged, the first thing to remember is not to try to start it,” warns Dr. Morgan. “The car should be inspected by a qualified professional who can safely disconnect the battery and assess any damage.” After a flood, it’s also a good idea to contact your car’s manufacturer or an authorized dealer for guidance on how to proceed. They may recommend that the battery be removed and replaced or that the entire vehicle undergo a thorough evaluation.

**First Responders and Best Practices**  
Emergency crews have been adapting their protocols to deal with E.V. fires. Many fire departments now carry specialized equipment and have training sessions focused on electric vehicle incidents. “We’re learning all the time,” says Conway. “As E.V. adoption grows, we’re updating our training manuals, investing in new tools, and working closely with automakers and battery manufacturers. Our goal is to handle these situations safely and efficiently.”

In some cases, submerged E.V.s that have not yet ignited are being moved to isolated areas where they can be monitored. By keeping them away from other vehicles and structures, any potential fire can be contained quickly. Some departments have even taken to placing E.V.s in large containers of water to cool the battery and prevent thermal runaway, though this is considered a last-resort measure.

**The Industry Response**  
Automakers are also taking steps to reduce the likelihood of battery fires caused by flooding. Improved sealants and battery enclosures are being developed to limit water intrusion, and companies are working closely with researchers to create safer battery chemistries.

“These events provide valuable lessons,” says Dr. Morgan. “Manufacturers are examining battery packs for better sealing technologies and using coatings that can prevent saltwater from reaching critical components. The industry is invested in making E.V.s as resilient and safe as possible, even under extreme conditions.”

**A Rare but Manageable Concern**  
For prospective and current E.V. owners, the bottom line is that while saltwater submersion can lead to battery fires, these situations remain uncommon. With the proper precautions—such as moving your vehicle to safer ground before a storm and seeking professional help if it’s flooded—most risks can be minimized. As engineers, scientists, and firefighters continue to adapt and improve safety measures, the fear of an E.V. catching fire in the aftermath of a hurricane is likely to diminish even further.

“E.V.s represent a shift toward a cleaner, more sustainable transportation system,” says Conway. “Understanding their unique characteristics helps us be better prepared. As we learn more, we’ll keep refining our response and prevention strategies, ensuring that E.V.s are as safe as they are innovative.”
