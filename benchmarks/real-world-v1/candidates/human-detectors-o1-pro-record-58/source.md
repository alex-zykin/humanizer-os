A San Francisco startup aiming to help dogs live longer, healthier lives has just raised $45 million in funding, highlighting a growing interest among venture-capital firms in longevity technologies—this time, not just for humans. The company, named Loyal, is developing drugs intended to slow the effects of aging in dogs and, if successful, could open the door to new approaches in age-related care for pets.

“We love our dogs like family members,” says Celine Halioua, Loyal’s founder and CEO, “but they simply don’t live as long as we want them to. At Loyal, we’re trying to address the underlying biology of aging so that our pets can enjoy more happy and active years with us.”

Founded in 2019, Loyal has drawn attention from high-profile investors as the pet industry continues to expand. According to the American Pet Products Association, Americans spend more than $100 billion a year on their pets, reflecting a strong appetite for products and services that improve animal health and longevity. With this latest round of funding, Loyal plans to continue its research, run clinical trials, and prepare the groundwork for bringing its first products to market.

The startup’s core idea is to develop pharmaceuticals that target the biological mechanisms of aging. Instead of addressing one disease at a time—such as arthritis or heart conditions—Loyal hopes to treat aging itself as a risk factor for multiple diseases. If successful, the drugs could extend not only the length of dogs’ lives, but also the quality of those extra years.

Dr. Matt Kaeberlein, a longevity researcher at the University of Washington who is not affiliated with Loyal, notes that the concept of treating aging as a medical condition is gaining traction in the scientific community. “The past decade has shown us that aging is modifiable,” he says. “If we can slow down the aging process, we may be able to significantly reduce the incidence of age-related diseases. Doing this for dogs is an appealing and achievable first step, given their relatively short lifespans and the strength of owner interest.”

Venture-capital investors have been paying close attention. While several longevity startups focus on human aging, the pet market offers a unique entry point. Dogs have shorter lifespans than people, meaning that clinical trials can yield data more quickly. The pet health market is also less strictly regulated than human pharmaceuticals, potentially speeding up the time it takes for a product to reach consumers. “Investors see an opportunity to generate returns while contributing to a growing field with vast potential,” says Mark Davies, a biotech investment analyst.

Loyal’s funding round places it among a cohort of longevity-focused startups that have recently raised significant capital. These companies aim to tap into a future where therapies could prevent or slow degenerative conditions across species. Some industry watchers believe that if these therapies are successful in dogs, the lessons learned could guide similar approaches for human medicine. “There’s a lot we can learn about aging from animal models,” Dr. Kaeberlein explains. “Dogs share our environment and many of our health risks, making them an ideal species to study.”

Still, the journey from concept to commercialization is long. Drug development is fraught with uncertainty, clinical trials can take years, and not every promising idea leads to a safe and effective product. Loyal must navigate a complex web of scientific, regulatory, and market challenges. “It’s one thing to prove something works in a laboratory setting,” says Davies, “and another to demonstrate safety, efficacy, and reliability at scale.”

For now, Loyal’s investors remain optimistic. The funding round, led by prominent venture firms, suggests that even with the known challenges, the company’s approach resonates with those holding the purse strings. “We look for investments that can reshape markets,” says an unnamed investor involved in the deal. “If Loyal’s approach pans out, it could fundamentally change how we think about pet health and aging.”

In the meantime, pet owners eagerly follow these developments. While it may be several years before a longevity treatment hits store shelves or veterinarians’ offices, the idea that science could help extend the time we spend with our furry friends is appealing. “Dogs bring us so much joy,” says Halioua. “Why not give them the healthiest, longest lives we can?”

As research continues, Loyal’s latest funding round signals a broader shift in how we view aging—not just as an inevitable decline, but as a biological process we might someday influence. For dogs, and perhaps one day for humans, that shift could mean more vibrant years together.
