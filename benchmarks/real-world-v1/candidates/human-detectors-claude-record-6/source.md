The Le Griffon, built by French explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle in 1679, holds the distinction of being the first full-sized sailing ship on the upper Great Lakes. It's also earned another, more dubious title: the most claimed shipwreck discovery in North American history. Despite numerous reported findings over the past century, the authentic remains of this historic vessel continue to elude researchers and explorers.<br><br>"The Le Griffon represents the holy grail of Great Lakes shipwrecks," says Dr. James Delgado, marine archaeologist and former director of NOAA's Maritime Heritage Program. "Its historical significance, combined with its mysterious disappearance, has created an almost mythical status around this vessel."<br><br>The ship's story began with promise but ended in tragedy. La Salle commissioned the Le Griffon's construction to support his fur trading enterprise and exploration of the Great Lakes region. The 45-foot vessel, adorned with a carved griffin on its bow, set sail from Niagara Falls in August 1679 with a crew of six. After successfully navigating Lake Erie and Lake Huron, the ship disappeared during its return journey on Lake Michigan, marking one of the earliest recorded shipwrecks in Great Lakes history.<br><br>Dr. Carrie Sowden, archaeological director at the National Museum of the Great Lakes, explains why finding the genuine wreck has proved so challenging: "Lake Michigan's vast size, coupled with the technological limitations of the 17th century, means we have only approximate coordinates for where the ship might have gone down. Additionally, the lake's cold, fresh water can preserve wooden wrecks remarkably well, making it difficult to distinguish between vessels of different eras."<br><br>Since the early 1900s, dozens of divers and explorers have claimed to have found the Le Griffon. Most recently, in 2014, Michigan-based treasure hunters Kevin Dykstra and Frederick Monroe announced they had located the wreck near Frankfort, Michigan. However, like all previous claims, this one failed to withstand expert scrutiny.<br><br>"Many people underestimate the complexity of properly identifying a historical shipwreck," says Dr. Kenneth Vrana, director of the Center for Maritime and Underwater Resource Management. "It requires a combination of archaeological expertise, historical documentation, and scientific analysis. Simply finding old wooden timbers underwater isn't enough."<br><br>The challenge of authentication is compounded by Lake Michigan's rich maritime history. The lake bottom is littered with thousands of shipwrecks from various periods, many sharing similar construction characteristics. "We estimate there are more than 1,500 documented shipwrecks in Lake Michigan alone," says Brendon Baillod, Great Lakes maritime historian. "Many of these were wooden schooners that, to the untrained eye, might resemble a 17th-century vessel like the Le Griffon."<br><br>Modern technology has both helped and hindered the search. While sonar scanning and remote-operated vehicles have made it easier to locate underwater objects, these tools have also led to increased false positives. Social media and internet platforms have amplified unverified claims, making it harder to separate genuine research from speculation.<br><br>"The proliferation of diving technology has democratized shipwreck exploration, which is generally positive," says Dr. Delgado. "However, it's crucial to maintain scientific rigor in the identification process. The archaeological community has established protocols for shipwreck authentication that must be followed."<br><br>These protocols include detailed documentation of the wreck site, analysis of construction techniques and materials, and correlation with historical records. Carbon dating, while useful, isn't always definitive for ships from this period due to what scientists call the "marine reservoir effect," which can skew results.<br><br>The search continues to captivate both professionals and amateurs. The Michigan Department of Natural Resources maintains strict guidelines for shipwreck exploration, requiring permits for any significant disturbance of potential historic sites. This protection helps preserve archaeological integrity but can frustrate eager explorers.<br><br>"We're not trying to discourage discovery," says Sandra Clark, director of the Michigan History Center. "We're ensuring that when the Le Griffon is finally found, it's properly documented and preserved for future generations."<br><br>The true location of the Le Griffon remains one of the Great Lakes' most enduring mysteries. Some experts speculate it may have been destroyed by waves and ice over the centuries, leaving little to find. Others believe it lies preserved in deep water, waiting to be discovered by someone with the right combination of technology, expertise, and luck.<br><br>"Whatever its fate, the Le Griffon's story reminds us of the Great Lakes' rich maritime heritage," says Baillod. "It represents not just a shipwreck, but the beginning of European commercial navigation on the upper Great Lakes."<br><br>Until definitive evidence emerges, the search will undoubtedly continue, drawing new generations of explorers to Lake Michigan's waters. For now, the Le Griffon maintains its status as the most elusive shipwreck in North American history, its true location still hidden beneath the waves, despite the many claims to its discovery.
