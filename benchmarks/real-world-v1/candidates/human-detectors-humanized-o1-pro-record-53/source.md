A bale of hay might look traditional enough, but on a ranch near Camarillo, California, a few Angus steers have been munching something extra in their daily feed: dried seaweed pellets. The unlikely ingredient isn’t a culinary stunt; it’s part of a serious effort to clean up the beef industry’s image—literally. According to a new study led by researchers at the University of California, Davis, adding small amounts of a certain red seaweed to grazing cattle’s diet cut their methane emissions by nearly 40 percent.

For years, scientists have been trying to find ways to reduce livestock-generated methane, a potent greenhouse gas that traps far more heat per unit than carbon dioxide. Although methane doesn’t linger as long in the atmosphere, it packs an outsized punch in the short term, and cattle are notorious for producing it in their rumen as they digest feed. Many proposed fixes have fallen flat, but feeding seaweed—specifically Asparagopsis taxiformis—appears to tamp down certain microbes in a cow’s gut, thus lowering methane belches without harming the animal’s appetite or growth.

“These results surprised us because we tested the additive in a realistic grazing setting, not just in feedlots,” says Ermias Kebreab, an animal scientist at UC Davis and one of the study’s authors. “We had good indications from controlled trials, but we didn’t know how well it would work when cattle roamed on pastureland.” Previous experiments in confined environments showed even larger methane reductions, sometimes more than 50 or 60 percent. Grazing conditions present a tougher challenge, since cows wander and forage at will. Yet the new field trial still recorded an impressive drop in emissions—roughly 30 to 40 percent—over several months.

To apply the treatment, technicians ground the seaweed into powder and compressed it into pellets that could be easily mixed with molasses and other supplements. The cows didn’t seem to mind the taste. In fact, the animals gained weight at rates similar to their herdmates that didn’t eat seaweed, suggesting no loss of feed efficiency. “We were particularly encouraged that animal health and productivity remained steady,” says Breanna Roque, a ruminant nutrition researcher at UC Davis who helped conduct the feeding trials. “We didn’t want a situation where we fixed one problem but caused another, like reduced growth or lower-quality meat.”

Ranchers who followed the study closely say it’s a welcome development, though they remain cautious about scaling it up. Demand for beef is as strong as ever, and producers are under pressure to shrink their environmental footprint. “We’re always told to cut back greenhouse gases, but it’s tough to do without concrete solutions,” says Austin Ramirez, who manages a family-run ranch in the Central Valley. “If seaweed pellets give us a practical tool, that’s worth exploring. Of course, we’ll have to see if the supply chain can handle it and what it costs.”

Cost is no small matter. While researchers didn’t release detailed pricing, growing and harvesting enough Asparagopsis on a commercial scale could prove a bottleneck. The red seaweed thrives in warm, tropical waters, and most of the current supply comes from pilot farms experimenting with cultivation. Still, a handful of companies have sprung up recently, betting that ranchers—and, indirectly, consumers—will pay a slight premium for more climate-friendly beef.

The environmental benefits could be significant. Methane from livestock is a major contributor to agricultural emissions, and curbing it would help countries meet their climate goals. Although cutting methane alone can’t solve global warming, it can buy time, experts argue. “Reducing livestock methane offers a relatively quick win,” says Frank Mitloehner, an air quality specialist at UC Davis who was not involved in this particular study but has researched greenhouse gases for decades. “If we can cut that emission source even by a modest percentage, the climate math gets a little easier in the near term.”

There are questions beyond economics and supply. For instance, how will consumers feel about cows eating seaweed? Surveys suggest that many people wouldn’t mind, as long as it doesn’t alter the flavor or safety of the beef. Preliminary taste tests show no detectable differences, and the FDA has taken a generally positive stance toward such feed additives, though more research might be required before any official green light. That said, the ranching industry can be slow to adopt new methods. Some producers wonder if dried seaweed pellets will be practical in remote pastures or if weather conditions might pose storage challenges.

The study’s authors acknowledge these hurdles. “It’s early days,” Roque says. “We’re encouraged by the data, but we know wide adoption means working out logistics and showing ranchers that it’s worth their while.” Additional long-term trials are planned to monitor animal health, productivity, and the subtle complexities of managing seaweed diets at scale.

For now, the findings show that a modest sprinkle of seaweed can shift the environmental balance in the cattle industry’s favor. The scientists behind the research stress that it won’t solve climate change on its own, but the next time you see cattle grazing quietly on rolling hills, it’s possible a few are sampling seaweed-laced pellets—an underappreciated twist in the quest to reduce agriculture’s carbon hoofprint.
