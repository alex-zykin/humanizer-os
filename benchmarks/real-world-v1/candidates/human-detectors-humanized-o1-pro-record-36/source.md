A box of old wheat seeds, collected in the early 1900s from family farms and tucked away in a seed vault for decades, might hold secrets that modern agriculture sorely needs. As the world’s population grows and unpredictable weather challenges today’s high-yield wheat, researchers are looking back in time to find varieties better suited for tomorrow’s dinner plates.

These long-forgotten seeds hail from a time before widespread synthetic fertilizers and uniform breeding programs. Back then, farmers relied on wheat that could thrive in local soils and adapt to whatever pests, diseases, and unpredictable weather nature threw at it. Now, wheat scientists are using these dormant strains — some more than a century old — to restore lost genetic traits that newer, more standardized varieties lack.

“It’s like finding a hidden toolkit buried in an old farmhouse attic,” says Ruth Gray, a seed conservation specialist at the Kew Millennium Seed Bank in England. “You open the box and realize these old seeds carry traits that could help wheat endure harsher conditions, resist diseases, or even produce stable harvests when the weather won’t cooperate.” Gray and her colleagues have been examining old wheat varieties gathered from small-scale farms in places like Russia, Turkey, and the U.S. Great Plains. One such variety is Turkey Red, brought to North America by Mennonite immigrants in the late 19th century. It’s known for deep roots that help it access moisture far below the soil surface and for steady yields even in seasons that swing between floods and droughts.

Modern high-yield wheat strains were bred for uniform fields, abundant chemical inputs, and relatively predictable growing seasons. That worked well for decades, feeding millions as agriculture intensified. But as farmers now face erratic rainfall, scorching heatwaves, and emerging diseases, these streamlined varieties sometimes falter. In contrast, older wheat lines often contain overlooked genetic quirks: a tougher stem, a thicker leaf coating that repels certain pests, or the ability to flower just when a brief period of spring rain arrives.

“When we talk about global food security, wheat is at the heart of the conversation,” says Javier Montoya, a wheat breeder based in Mexico who collaborates with smallholder farmers. “Half the world’s population depends on wheat in some form. If we lose diversity in the gene pool, we risk losing resilience.” Montoya points out that heritage seeds can help restore that resilience, since farmers need grains that can flourish in all sorts of circumstances.

One recent trial in Nebraska paired a classic variety like Turkey Red with a modern wheat strain known for strong yields but poor drought tolerance. By crossing them, agronomists managed to produce offspring that carried better heat tolerance without sacrificing productivity. Early results showed that in a season of erratic rainfall, the hybrid wheat outperformed its modern parent by a meaningful margin. “This is not about chasing some antique romance,” says Michael Hopkins, an agronomist at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln who worked on the trial. “We’re applying good genetics and careful field testing to build a better, more stable crop.”

Farmers in parts of North Africa and South Asia are especially interested in these old strains. Many of these regions face seasonal dryness and intense heat that can push modern high-yield wheat past its breaking point. But older lines, originally grown in similar conditions, may handle stress better. Some of these heritage seeds carry genes that help plants slow growth when heat spikes, conserving energy and water until conditions improve. Others have deep, branching root systems that can tap moisture long after shallower-rooted plants have withered.

Seed banks around the globe, from the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway to the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s National Small Grains Collection in Idaho, have begun cataloging and distributing samples of these old wheat lines to researchers. But reintroducing them into modern agriculture isn’t straightforward. Many older strains produce lower yields than today’s commercial varieties under favorable conditions, or they might be difficult to harvest with modern machinery. That’s why breeders typically blend old and new lines, using advanced genetic tools to track which traits pass on to the next generation.

Adapting old wheat genetics to modern fields also requires working closely with farmers. Some growers in Italy’s Tuscany region, for instance, have reintroduced old “heritage” wheats that produce unique flavors prized by artisan bakers. Though yields might be modest, these grains fetch higher prices. Meanwhile, researchers examining these Italian strains have discovered traits that might boost disease resistance in other, more mainstream lines. It’s a win-win: preserving a culinary tradition while uncovering features that strengthen the broader wheat supply.

Not everyone believes heritage seeds are the final answer. Some worry about the time and resources needed to incorporate these older traits into widely grown varieties. Others note that while old genetic lines can add toughness, problems like poor soil health or lack of proper irrigation can’t be solved by genetics alone. But few dispute that more diversity in the wheat gene pool is a good idea, particularly when the world’s wheat fields are expected to feed over nine billion people in the coming decades.

Gray, who still marvels at the century-old seeds she handles in the storage rooms at Kew, says it best: “These old wheat varieties are a reminder that solutions aren’t always new. Sometimes they’ve been waiting quietly, sealed in a paper envelope, ready for a future we could never have imagined when they were first harvested.”
